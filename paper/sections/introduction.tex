\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Researchers often fit one linear regression to data pooled across observed groups, such as age categories, regions, treatment arms, or demographic groups. Pooling uses all available observations and can improve precision. It also imposes a substantive restriction: the resulting coefficient vector must serve as an adequate summary for all groups. If the underlying relationships differ, a pooled coefficient may describe no group particularly well. If groups are small or have different covariate distributions, subgroup estimates may also disagree even when their population slopes are the same.

This distinction matters because coefficient disagreement has several possible explanations. Under correct linear specification with common slopes, changing the covariate distribution affects the precision of subgroup estimates through the information matrices $X_g^\top X_g$, but it does not by itself change the conditional expectation of the subgroup OLS slope. Under misspecification, however, an OLS coefficient is a best linear approximation that depends on the regressor distribution \citep{berk2019}. In that setting, distributional differences can change the population linear approximation even without a change in the underlying conditional mean function. A pooled analysis therefore requires attention both to sampling variation and to the relation between the fitted linear model and the covariate distribution.

Existing approaches address related but narrower questions. Structural-break procedures study coefficient stability in ordered data \citep{garbade1977,watsenengle1985}. Heterogeneous-treatment-effect methods examine whether a particular treatment effect varies across groups \citep{hte_ref}. \citet{tsoi2026} compare interaction and stratified regressions for subgroup treatment-effect analyses. Stable regression methods seek coefficients that are less sensitive to random splits \citep{bertsimas2020}, while latent-group models estimate unobserved group structure \citep{liu2020}. The present setting is different: group membership is observed, the model is cross-sectional, and the question is whether an already fitted pooled regression remains an adequate descriptive and predictive summary within those groups.

This paper develops a finite-sample diagnostic study of that question. We compare pooled and subgroup-specific ordinary least squares fits using five quantities: sign stability, standardized coefficient drift, context-normalized drift, the pooled-versus-subgroup prediction gap, and covariate shift. A controlled simulation separates finite-sample variation, covariate shift, coefficient heterogeneity, and their combination. An age-stratified empirical illustration then shows how the diagnostics behave in observed data. The contribution is diagnostic rather than estimative. It does not introduce a new regression estimator or establish a causal test for effect modification.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} defines the diagnostics and model comparisons. Section~\ref{sec:simulation} describes the simulation and its results. Section~\ref{sec:realdata} presents the empirical illustration. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the mathematical interpretation and limitations.
